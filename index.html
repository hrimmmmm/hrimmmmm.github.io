    Mistake Makes Meaning

    Stable Diffusion, Parameter manipulation, Digital image series

    2023-2026

    Generative AI systems are increasingly optimized to produce images that appear accurate, realistic, and aligned with human expectations. But who determines what makes an image correct? In Mistake Makes Meaning, I deliberately manipulate Stable Diffusion's generation parameters to produce distorted bodies, impossible movements, and unexpected visual forms. Rather than correcting or discarding these outputs, I collect and reinterpret them as artistic material.
    Drawing on Rosa Menkman's exploration of technological glitches, the project considers error not only as a limitation of the machine but also as a reflection of the standards through which humans judge images. These distortions make visible the expectations embedded in generative systems while opening up alternative visual possibilities.

    The Trained Gaze

    TouchDesigner, ComfyUI - 9 min 31 sec · Single-channel moving image, presented on a 75m media wall

    2025

    Is the machine's gaze really its own? The Trained Gaze investigates how the visual conventions through which humans represent women become embedded in generative AI systems. I collected recurring representations of women circulating online and used them as inputs for a generative process, producing new images that reflect and transform those visual patterns. The resulting moving-image work explores a feedback loop in which human-made representations shape machine-generated imagery, which in turn becomes part of our visual culture.
    Drawing on Laura Mulvey's critique of the gaze and Trevor Paglen and Kate Crawford's investigations of machine vision and classification, the work questions whether machine perception can ever be separated from the human assumptions on which it is trained.

    JOKBO 족보

    StyleGAN, HTML/CSS/JavaScript, interactive website

    2026

    What would the family tree of an AI-generated person look like? JOKBO is an interactive website that constructs fictional genealogies for people who have never existed. Starting from a generated face, visitors can search for its "parents," which are selected through visual similarity in StyleGAN's latent space. Each parent can then be traced further, producing an expanding family tree without any biological ancestry.
    The project borrows the structure of jokbo (족보), traditional Korean genealogical records, and applies it to computationally generated identities. By transforming visual resemblance into a fictional form of inheritance, the work questions how familiar categories such as parent, ancestor, and relative acquire their meaning. The project explores what happens when a system designed to document biological relationships is applied to identities that exist only as generated images.

    Thank yuo for your email

    Participatory project · 40 handwritten letters and annotations

    2026

    As AI-generated writing becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish from human expression, what makes a text feel unmistakably human?
    In Thank yuo for your email, I invited 40 university students to write handwritten thank-you letters without AI assistance and consider how they could demonstrate their human authorship. The letters were anonymized and redistributed, and participants were asked to mark the passages they perceived as particularly human. The project brings together handwritten expressions, perceived imperfections, and readers' annotations to examine how people recognize human presence in writing. Typos, awkward phrasing, and unexpected expressions become possible signs of authenticity rather than errors to be corrected. Through these differing interpretations, the work questions how our standards for recognizing human authorship are changing alongside generative AI.

    Unspoken into the Spoken

    Generative AI, participatory diary study

    Research published in International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction - 2026

    How can two people experience the same situation yet understand it differently? In a six-day diary study with seven intercultural couples, I explored how AI-generated imagery could help participants express cultural differences that were difficult to articulate in words. Participants translated personal experiences into images and used these visual interpretations as starting points for conversations with their partners.
    Rather than using AI to produce a single, shared representation, the study examined how different interpretations could coexist and become visible through image-making and dialogue. The project extends my interest in classification and interpretation into interpersonal relationships, exploring how technology can surface differences in meaning rather than resolve them into a single understanding.
